\title[On the Uniqueness of the Even Prime]
{On the Uniqueness of the Even Prime:\\
A Self-Contained Development from the Peano Axioms}

\author{}
\address{}
\email{}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
We give a fully self-contained proof that \(2\) is the unique even prime
natural number. Starting from the Peano axioms alone, we prove a recursion
lemma, construct addition and multiplication, develop the usual order and
the well-ordering principle, and establish every arithmetic and
divisibility lemma subsequently required. We then define primes, composite
numbers, and even numbers, prove that \(2\) itself is an even prime, and
show that there is no even prime strictly less than \(2\) and no even
prime strictly greater than \(2\). Combining these facts yields the
uniqueness theorem. Five independent proofs of the ``greater than \(2\)''
half are recorded, each resting only on results proved earlier in the
paper. No external theorem of number theory is used as a black box.
\end{abstract}

\subjclass[2020]{Primary 11A41; Secondary 03B30, 11A51}

divisibility, elementary number theory, self-contained proof}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The assertion that \(2\) is the only even prime number is among the most
frequently quoted facts of elementary arithmetic. Precisely stated, it
reads:
\begin{quote}
\textit{If \(p\) is a prime natural number and \(2\) divides \(p\), then
\(p = 2\).}
\end{quote}
Despite its familiarity, a fully rigorous account requires more
infrastructure than is often written down. One must specify what a
natural number is, what addition and multiplication are, what it means
for one natural number to divide another, and what the words
\emph{prime} and \emph{even} denote. Each of these notions rests on prior
definitions or axioms, and each intermediate claim used in the uniqueness
argument must itself be proved.

The present paper supplies that account. We adopt the Peano axioms as our
foundation (\Cref{sec:peano}), construct addition, multiplication, and
order, and prove the order-theoretic and arithmetic lemmas that the later
arguments invoke (\Cref{sec:arith,sec:order}). We then develop
divisibility (\Cref{sec:div}), define primes, composite numbers, and even
numbers (\Cref{sec:prime}), and prove a sequence of preparatory lemmas,
including the fact that \(2\) is itself an even prime
(\Cref{sec:prep}). Only then do we establish the two complementary
halves of the uniqueness statement---that no even prime is strictly less
than \(2\) (\Cref{sec:below}) and that no even prime is strictly greater
than \(2\) (\Cref{sec:above})---and assemble them into the grand theorem
(\Cref{sec:grand}).

Throughout, every inference is justified by a previously numbered axiom,
definition, or lemma. We do not rely on unspoken ``obvious'' arithmetic
facts.

\section{The Peano axioms}
\label{sec:peano}

We work throughout in classical first-order logic. The natural numbers
are introduced axiomatically.

\begin{definition}[Natural numbers]
\label{def:N}
We postulate a set \(\N\), a distinguished element \(0 \in \N\), and a
function \(\Suc \colon \N \to \N\) (the \emph{successor}), subject to
Axioms~\ref{ax:peano1},~\ref{ax:peano2}, and~\ref{ax:peano3} below. Elements of \(\N\) are called
\emph{natural numbers}, or simply \emph{numbers} when the context is
clear.
\end{definition}

\begin{axiom}[Zero is not a successor]
\label{ax:peano1}
For every \(n \in \N\), one has \(\Suc(n) \neq 0\).
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Injectivity of successor]
\label{ax:peano2}
For all \(m,n \in \N\), if \(\Suc(m) = \Suc(n)\), then \(m = n\).
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Induction]
\label{ax:peano3}
Let \(P\) be any property of natural numbers. Suppose
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \(P(0)\) holds, and
  \item for every \(n \in \N\), if \(P(n)\) holds then \(P(\Suc(n))\) holds.
\end{enumerate}
Then \(P(n)\) holds for every \(n \in \N\).
\end{axiom}

\begin{notation}
\label{not:numerals}
We write
\[
  1 \eqdef \Suc(0),\qquad
  2 \eqdef \Suc(1) = \Suc(\Suc(0)),\qquad
  3 \eqdef \Suc(2),\qquad
  4 \eqdef \Suc(3),
\]
and so on for further numerals as needed.
\end{notation}

\begin{lemma}[Zero and one are distinct]
\label{lem:0neq1}
One has \(0 \neq 1\) and \(1 \neq 2\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If \(0 = 1 = \Suc(0)\), then \(\Suc(0) = 0\), contradicting
\Cref{ax:peano1}. If \(1 = 2\), then \(\Suc(0) = \Suc(1)\), so
\(0 = 1\) by~\Cref{ax:peano2}, which we have just ruled out.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Every nonzero number is a successor]
\label{lem:pred}
For every \(n \in \N\), either \(n = 0\) or there exists a unique
\(m \in \N\) such that \(n = \Suc(m)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let \(P(n)\) be the stated disjunction. Then \(P(0)\) holds by the first
alternative. If \(P(n)\) holds, then \(P(\Suc(n))\) holds by taking
\(m = n\) in the second alternative. By~\Cref{ax:peano3}, \(P(n)\) holds
for all \(n\). Uniqueness of \(m\) is~\Cref{ax:peano2}.
\end{proof}

% ============================== 3 ==============================
\section{Addition and multiplication}
\label{sec:arith}

\begin{lemma}[Recursion for a single sequence]
\label{lem:recursion}
Let \(a \in \N\) and let \(f \colon \N \to \N\) be any function. Then there
exists a unique function \(u \colon \N \to \N\) such that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recursion}
  u(0) = a
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  u(\Suc(n)) = f\bigl(u(n)\bigr)
  \quad\text{for every }n \in \N.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\textit{Uniqueness.}
Suppose \(u\) and \(v\) both satisfy~\eqref{eq:recursion}. Let \(P(n)\)
assert \(u(n) = v(n)\). Then \(P(0)\) holds because both equal \(a\). If
\(P(n)\) holds, then
\[
  u(\Suc(n)) = f\bigl(u(n)\bigr) = f\bigl(v(n)\bigr) = v(\Suc(n)).
\]
By~\Cref{ax:peano3}, \(u = v\).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Existence.}
We first show: for every \(n \in \N\), there exists a finite initial
segment realizing the recursion up to \(n\). More precisely, let
\(Q(n)\) assert that there exists a function
\(u_n \colon \{ k \in \N : k \le n \} \to \N\) such that
\(u_n(0) = a\) and \(u_n(\Suc(k)) = f(u_n(k))\) whenever
\(\Suc(k) \le n\). For \(n = 0\), take \(u_0(0) = a\). Assuming
\(Q(n)\) with witness \(u_n\), define \(u_{\Suc(n)}\) by
\(u_{\Suc(n)}(k) = u_n(k)\) for \(k \le n\) and
\(u_{\Suc(n)}(\Suc(n)) = f(u_n(n))\). This witnesses \(Q(\Suc(n))\).
By induction, \(Q(n)\) holds for all \(n\).

Moreover, the value \(u_n(n)\) is independent of the choice of witness:
if two segments agree with the recursion up to \(n\), then by the same
inductive argument as in the uniqueness paragraph they agree at every
\(k \le n\). Define \(u(n)\) to be this common value \(u_n(n)\). Then
\(u(0) = a\), and
\[
  u(\Suc(n)) = u_{\Suc(n)}(\Suc(n)) = f\bigl(u_n(n)\bigr) = f\bigl(u(n)\bigr).
\]
Thus \(u\) satisfies~\eqref{eq:recursion}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:recursion-set}
The preceding argument treats functions on initial segments as
mathematical objects. In a set-theoretic foundation this is
unproblematic; equivalently, one may read \(u_n\) as an
\((n+1)\)-tuple in \(\N^{n+1}\) and rewrite the proof in that language.
No property of \(\N\) beyond Axioms~\ref{ax:peano1},~\ref{ax:peano2}, and~\ref{ax:peano3} is
used.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Addition]
\label{def:add}
For each fixed \(m \in \N\), \Cref{lem:recursion} applied to \(a = m\) and
\(f = \Suc\) yields a unique function \(u_m \colon \N \to \N\) with
\(u_m(0) = m\) and \(u_m(\Suc(n)) = \Suc(u_m(n))\). Define
\[
  m + n \eqdef u_m(n).
\]
Writing the recursive clauses explicitly, addition is the unique binary
operation satisfying, for all \(m,n \in \N\),
\begin{align}
  m + 0 &= m, \label{eq:add0} \\
  m + \Suc(n) &= \Suc(m + n). \label{eq:addS}
\end{align}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Adding zero on the left]
\label{lem:0+n}
For every \(n \in \N\), one has \(0 + n = n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Proceed by induction on \(n\). For \(n = 0\),
\(0 + 0 = 0\) by~\eqref{eq:add0}. Assuming \(0 + n = n\), we obtain
\[
  0 + \Suc(n) = \Suc(0 + n) = \Suc(n)
\]
by~\eqref{eq:addS} and the inductive hypothesis. Apply~\Cref{ax:peano3}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Successor as adding one]
\label{lem:n+1}
For every \(n \in \N\), one has \(n + 1 = \Suc(n)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By~\Cref{not:numerals} and~\eqref{eq:addS}--\eqref{eq:add0},
\[
  n + 1 = n + \Suc(0) = \Suc(n + 0) = \Suc(n).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Associativity of addition]
\label{lem:add-assoc}
For all \(a,b,c \in \N\), one has \((a + b) + c = a + (b + c)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix \(a,b\) and induct on \(c\). For \(c = 0\), both sides equal
\(a + b\) by~\eqref{eq:add0}. Assuming the claim for \(c\), we have
\begin{align*}
  (a + b) + \Suc(c)
    &= \Suc\bigl((a + b) + c\bigr)
     = \Suc\bigl(a + (b + c)\bigr)
     = a + \Suc(b + c)
     = a + \bigl(b + \Suc(c)\bigr),
\end{align*}
using~\eqref{eq:addS} and the inductive hypothesis.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Commutativity of addition]
\label{lem:add-comm}
For all \(m,n \in \N\), one has \(m + n = n + m\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first claim that \(m + 1 = 1 + m\) for all \(m\). Induct on \(m\):
the case \(m = 0\) is~\Cref{lem:0+n,lem:n+1}. Assuming
\(m + 1 = 1 + m\),
\begin{align*}
  \Suc(m) + 1
    &= \Suc(m) + \Suc(0)
     = \Suc\bigl(\Suc(m) + 0\bigr)
     = \Suc(\Suc(m)) \\
    &= \Suc(m + 1)
     = \Suc(1 + m)
     = 1 + \Suc(m),
\end{align*}
where the penultimate step used the inductive hypothesis. Thus
\(m + 1 = 1 + m\) for all \(m\).

Now fix \(m\) and induct on \(n\) for the full claim. The case
\(n = 0\) is~\Cref{lem:0+n} and~\eqref{eq:add0}. Assuming
\(m + n = n + m\),
\begin{align*}
  m + \Suc(n)
    &= \Suc(m + n)
     = \Suc(n + m)
     = n + \Suc(m)
     = n + (m + 1)
     = n + (1 + m)
     = (n + 1) + m
     = \Suc(n) + m,
\end{align*}
using~\eqref{eq:addS}, the inductive hypothesis, \Cref{lem:n+1}, the
claim of the previous paragraph, and~\Cref{lem:add-assoc}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Multiplication]
\label{def:mul}
For each fixed \(m \in \N\), apply~\Cref{lem:recursion} with \(a = 0\) and
with \(f\) equal to the map \(x \mapsto x + m\) (already constructed
in~\Cref{def:add}). The resulting unique function \(v_m\) satisfies
\(v_m(0) = 0\) and \(v_m(\Suc(n)) = v_m(n) + m\). Define
\[
  m \cdot n \eqdef v_m(n).
\]
Equivalently, multiplication is the unique binary operation satisfying,
for all \(m,n \in \N\),
\begin{align}
  m \cdot 0 &= 0, \label{eq:mul0} \\
  m \cdot \Suc(n) &= (m \cdot n) + m. \label{eq:mulS}
\end{align}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Multiplication by zero on the left]
\label{lem:0n}
For every \(n \in \N\), one has \(0 \cdot n = 0\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induct on \(n\). The case \(n = 0\) is~\eqref{eq:mul0}. Assuming
\(0 \cdot n = 0\),
\[
  0 \cdot \Suc(n) = (0 \cdot n) + 0 = 0 + 0 = 0
\]
by~\eqref{eq:mulS}, the inductive hypothesis, and~\eqref{eq:add0}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Multiplication by one]
\label{lem:n1}
For every \(n \in \N\), one has \(n \cdot 1 = n\) and \(1 \cdot n = n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First,
\[
  n \cdot 1 = n \cdot \Suc(0) = (n \cdot 0) + n = 0 + n = n
\]
by~\eqref{eq:mulS},~\eqref{eq:mul0}, and~\Cref{lem:0+n}. For the second
identity, induct on \(n\): the case \(n = 0\) is~\eqref{eq:mul0}; assuming
\(1 \cdot n = n\),
\[
  1 \cdot \Suc(n) = (1 \cdot n) + 1 = n + 1 = \Suc(n)
\]
by~\eqref{eq:mulS}, the inductive hypothesis, and~\Cref{lem:n+1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Distributivity]
\label{lem:distrib}
For all \(a,b,c \in \N\),
\[
  a \cdot (b + c) = (a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  (a + b) \cdot c = (a \cdot c) + (b \cdot c).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For the first identity, fix \(a,b\) and induct on \(c\). The case
\(c = 0\) is immediate from~\eqref{eq:add0} and~\eqref{eq:mul0}. Assuming
the claim for \(c\),
\begin{align*}
  a \cdot \bigl(b + \Suc(c)\bigr)
    &= a \cdot \Suc(b + c)
     = \bigl(a \cdot (b + c)\bigr) + a \\
    &= \bigl((a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c)\bigr) + a
     = (a \cdot b) + \bigl((a \cdot c) + a\bigr)
     = (a \cdot b) + \bigl(a \cdot \Suc(c)\bigr),
\end{align*}
using~\eqref{eq:addS},~\eqref{eq:mulS}, the inductive hypothesis,
and~\Cref{lem:add-assoc}.

For the second identity, fix \(a,b\) and induct on \(c\). The case
\(c = 0\) is~\eqref{eq:mul0} and~\eqref{eq:add0}. Assuming the claim for
\(c\),
\begin{align*}
  (a + b) \cdot \Suc(c)
    &= \bigl((a + b) \cdot c\bigr) + (a + b)
     = \bigl((a \cdot c) + (b \cdot c)\bigr) + (a + b) \\
    &= (a \cdot c) + \bigl((b \cdot c) + (a + b)\bigr)
     = (a \cdot c) + \bigl(((b \cdot c) + b) + a\bigr) \\
    &= \bigl((a \cdot c) + a\bigr) + \bigl((b \cdot c) + b\bigr)
     = \bigl(a \cdot \Suc(c)\bigr) + \bigl(b \cdot \Suc(c)\bigr),
\end{align*}
where we have used~\eqref{eq:mulS}, the inductive hypothesis,
\Cref{lem:add-assoc,lem:add-comm} repeatedly to rearrange sums.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Commutativity of multiplication]
\label{lem:mul-comm}
For all \(m,n \in \N\), one has \(m \cdot n = n \cdot m\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First, \(m \cdot 0 = 0 = 0 \cdot m\) by~\eqref{eq:mul0}
and~\Cref{lem:0n}. Next we claim \(m \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot m\), which
is~\Cref{lem:n1}. Now fix \(m\) and induct on \(n\). The base
\(n = 0\) is settled. Assuming \(m \cdot n = n \cdot m\),
\begin{align*}
  m \cdot \Suc(n)
    &= (m \cdot n) + m
     = (n \cdot m) + m
     = (n \cdot m) + (1 \cdot m)
     = (n + 1) \cdot m
     = \Suc(n) \cdot m,
\end{align*}
using~\eqref{eq:mulS}, the inductive hypothesis, \Cref{lem:n1},
\Cref{lem:distrib}, and~\Cref{lem:n+1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Associativity of multiplication]
\label{lem:mul-assoc}
For all \(a,b,c \in \N\), one has \((a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix \(a,b\) and induct on \(c\). For \(c = 0\) both sides are \(0\).
Assuming the claim for \(c\),
\begin{align*}
  (a \cdot b) \cdot \Suc(c)
    &= \bigl((a \cdot b) \cdot c\bigr) + (a \cdot b)
     = \bigl(a \cdot (b \cdot c)\bigr) + (a \cdot b)
     = a \cdot \bigl((b \cdot c) + b\bigr)
     = a \cdot \bigl(b \cdot \Suc(c)\bigr),
\end{align*}
by~\eqref{eq:mulS}, the inductive hypothesis, and~\Cref{lem:distrib}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Two times \(n\)]
\label{lem:2n}
For every \(n \in \N\), one has \(2 \cdot n = n + n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induct on \(n\). For \(n = 0\), both sides equal \(0\). Assuming the
claim for \(n\),
\begin{align*}
  2 \cdot \Suc(n)
    &= (2 \cdot n) + 2
     = (n + n) + (1 + 1)
     = \bigl((n + n) + 1\bigr) + 1
     = \bigl((n + 1) + n\bigr) + 1
     = (n + 1) + (n + 1)
     = \Suc(n) + \Suc(n),
\end{align*}
using~\eqref{eq:mulS}, the inductive hypothesis, \Cref{not:numerals},
\Cref{lem:add-assoc,lem:add-comm,lem:n+1}.
\end{proof}

% ============================== 4 ==============================
\section{Order}
\label{sec:order}

\begin{definition}[Order]
\label{def:order}
For \(m,n \in \N\), we write \(m \le n\) if and only if there exists
\(k \in \N\) such that \(m + k = n\). We write \(m < n\) if and only if
\(m \le n\) and \(m \neq n\). We write \(m \ge n\) (resp.\ \(m > n\)) if
and only if \(n \le m\) (resp.\ \(n < m\)).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Order basics]
\label{lem:order-basic}
Let \(a,b,c \in \N\).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \(a \le a\).
  \item If \(a \le b\) and \(b \le c\), then \(a \le c\).
  \item \(a \le b\) if and only if \(a + c \le b + c\).
  \item \(a < \Suc(a)\).
  \item \(0 \le a\).
  \item \(a < b\) if and only if there exists \(k \in \N\) with
        \(k \neq 0\) and \(a + k = b\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i)~Take \(k = 0\) in~\Cref{def:order}, using~\eqref{eq:add0}.
(ii)~If \(a + k = b\) and \(b + \ell = c\), then
\(a + (k + \ell) = c\) by~\Cref{lem:add-assoc}.
(iii)~If \(a + k = b\), then \((a + c) + k = (b + c)\) by
\Cref{lem:add-assoc,lem:add-comm}. Conversely, if
\((a + c) + k = b + c\), the same rearrangement yields \(a + k = b\)
after cancelling \(c\), which is legitimate by the following observation:
if \(x + c = y + c\), then \(x = y\). (Prove the observation by induction
on \(c\): trivial for \(c = 0\); if true for \(c\) and
\(x + \Suc(c) = y + \Suc(c)\), then \(\Suc(x + c) = \Suc(y + c)\), so
\(x + c = y + c\) by~\Cref{ax:peano2}, hence \(x = y\) by the inductive
hypothesis.)
(iv)~We have \(a + 1 = \Suc(a)\) by~\Cref{lem:n+1}, and
\(a \neq \Suc(a)\) because otherwise, writing \(P(n)\) for
``\(n \neq \Suc(n)\)'', one checks \(P(0)\) by~\Cref{ax:peano1} and the
inductive step by~\Cref{ax:peano2}, whence \(P(a)\)
by~\Cref{ax:peano3}. Thus \(a < \Suc(a)\).
(v)~Take \(k = a\) and apply~\Cref{lem:0+n}.
(vi)~If \(a < b\), then \(a + k = b\) for some \(k\), and \(k \neq 0\)
lest \(a = b\). Conversely, if \(a + k = b\) with \(k \neq 0\), then
\(a \le b\) and \(a \neq b\) (else \(k = 0\) by cancellation of \(a\)
against~\eqref{eq:add0}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Trichotomy]
\label{lem:trichotomy}
For all \(m,n \in \N\), exactly one of the following holds:
\(m < n\), or \(m = n\), or \(m > n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We first show that at least one holds. Fix \(m\) and let \(P(n)\) assert
the claim for the pair \((m,n)\). For \(n = 0\): either \(m = 0\), or
else \(m \neq 0\), so \(m = \Suc(m')\) for some \(m'\)
by~\Cref{lem:pred}, whence \(0 < m\) by repeatedly
applying~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(iv),(ii) (formally: \(0 \le m'\) by
\Cref{lem:order-basic}(v), so \(1 = \Suc(0) \le \Suc(m') = m\) by the
compatibility of \(\Suc\) with \(\le\), which follows from
\(x + k = y \Rightarrow \Suc(x) + k = \Suc(y)\); and \(0 \neq m\)).
Thus \(P(0)\) holds.

Assume \(P(n)\). If \(m < n\), then \(m < \Suc(n)\)
by~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(ii),(iv). If \(m = n\), then
\(m < \Suc(n)\) by~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(iv). If \(m > n\), write
\(m = n + k\) with \(k \neq 0\) by~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(vi). Then
\(k = \Suc(k')\) for some \(k'\) by~\Cref{lem:pred}, so
\[
  m = n + \Suc(k') = \Suc(n + k') = \Suc(n) + k',
\]
by~\eqref{eq:addS} and~\Cref{lem:add-comm},~\Cref{lem:n+1}. If
\(k' = 0\), then \(m = \Suc(n)\). If \(k' \neq 0\), then
\(m > \Suc(n)\). In all cases \(P(\Suc(n))\) holds. By induction,
trichotomy holds in the weak sense that at least one alternative obtains.

Exclusivity: if \(m < n\) and \(m = n\), contradict \(m \neq n\). If
\(m < n\) and \(n < m\), then \(m < m\) by transitivity, so
\(m + k = m\) for some \(k \neq 0\), whence \(k = 0\) by cancellation,
a contradiction. The remaining overlaps are symmetric.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Immediate predecessor bound]
\label{lem:lt-succ}
For every \(n \in \N\), the inequality \(n < 2\) holds if and only if
\(n = 0\) or \(n = 1\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If \(n = 0\), then \(0 < 1 < 2\) by~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(iv) twice and
transitivity, so \(0 < 2\). If \(n = 1\), then \(1 < 2\)
by~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(iv). Conversely, suppose \(n < 2\). By
trichotomy and~\Cref{lem:pred}, either \(n = 0\), or \(n = \Suc(m)\) for
a unique \(m\). In the latter case, \(m + 1 = n < 2 = 1 + 1\), so
\(m < 1\) after cancelling \(1\) (cf.\ the cancellation argument in
\Cref{lem:order-basic}(iii)). The only natural number strictly less
than \(1\) is \(0\): indeed, if \(m < 1\) and \(m \neq 0\), then
\(m = \Suc(m')\), so \(0 < m\) and hence \(1 = \Suc(0) \le m\),
contradicting \(m < 1\) by exclusivity of trichotomy. Thus \(m = 0\) and
\(n = 1\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Numbers greater than one]
\label{lem:gt1-ge2}
For every \(n \in \N\), one has \(n > 1\) if and only if \(n \ge 2\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If \(n \ge 2\), then either \(n = 2 > 1\) or \(n > 2 > 1\). Conversely,
if \(n > 1\), then \(n \neq 0\) and \(n \neq 1\) by exclusivity of
trichotomy (since \(0 < 1\)). By~\Cref{lem:lt-succ}, it is not the case
that \(n < 2\). By~\Cref{lem:trichotomy}, \(n \ge 2\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Well-ordering principle]
\label{thm:wellorder}
Let \(S \subseteq \N\) be a nonempty subset. Then \(S\) has a least
element: there exists \(m \in S\) such that \(m \le s\) for every
\(s \in S\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We prove the contrapositive form useful for induction. Let
\(P(n)\) be the statement: ``every subset of \(\N\) that contains an
element \(\le n\) has a least element.'' We show \(P(n)\) for all \(n\)
by induction.

For \(n = 0\): if \(S\) contains an element \(\le 0\), then that element
equals \(0\) by~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(v) and trichotomy, and \(0\) is
least in \(S\) because \(0 \le s\) for all \(s\)
by~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(v).

Assume \(P(n)\), and let \(S\) contain an element \(\le \Suc(n)\). If
\(S\) contains an element \(\le n\), we are done by \(P(n)\). Otherwise
every element of \(S\) is \(> n\), yet some element \(s\) satisfies
\(s \le \Suc(n)\). By trichotomy and \(s > n\), we get
\(s \ge \Suc(n)\), hence \(s = \Suc(n)\). We claim \(\Suc(n)\) is
least in \(S\): if \(t \in S\), then \(t > n\), so \(t \ge \Suc(n)\)
by the same reasoning. Thus \(P(\Suc(n))\) holds.

By~\Cref{ax:peano3}, \(P(n)\) holds for all \(n\). Now if
\(S \subseteq \N\) is nonempty, pick any \(s \in S\). Then \(S\)
contains an element \(\le s\), so \(S\) has a least element by \(P(s)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Least witness]
\label{cor:least-witness}
Let \(P\) be a property of natural numbers for which \(P(n_0)\) holds for
some \(n_0 \in \N\). Then there exists \(m \in \N\) such that \(P(m)\)
holds and \(P(k)\) fails for every \(k < m\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply~\Cref{thm:wellorder} to
\(S = \{ n \in \N : P(n)\}\), which is nonempty by hypothesis, and take
\(m\) to be its least element.
\end{proof}

\section{Divisibility}
\label{sec:div}

\begin{definition}[Divisibility]
\label{def:dvd}
Let \(d,n \in \N\). We say that \(d\) \emph{divides} \(n\), written
\(d \mid n\), if there exists \(k \in \N\) such that \(n = d \cdot k\).
In this case \(d\) is called a \emph{divisor} (or \emph{factor}) of
\(n\), and \(n\) is called a \emph{multiple} of \(d\).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Divisibility basics]
\label{lem:dvd-basic}
Let \(a,b,c \in \N\).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \(a \mid a\) and \(1 \mid a\) and \(a \mid 0\).
  \item If \(a \mid b\) and \(b \mid c\), then \(a \mid c\).
  \item If \(a \mid b\) and \(a \mid c\), then \(a \mid (b + c)\).
  \item If \(a \mid b\), then \(a \mid (b \cdot c)\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i)~We have \(a = a \cdot 1\) by~\Cref{lem:n1},
\(a = 1 \cdot a\) by~\Cref{lem:n1}, and \(0 = a \cdot 0\)
by~\eqref{eq:mul0}.
(ii)~If \(b = a \cdot k\) and \(c = b \cdot \ell\), then
\(c = a \cdot (k \cdot \ell)\) by~\Cref{lem:mul-assoc}.
(iii)~If \(b = a \cdot k\) and \(c = a \cdot \ell\), then
\(b + c = a \cdot (k + \ell)\) by~\Cref{lem:distrib}.
(iv)~If \(b = a \cdot k\), then
\(b \cdot c = a \cdot (k \cdot c)\) by~\Cref{lem:mul-assoc}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Positive divisors are positive]
\label{lem:dvd-pos}
If \(d \mid n\) and \(n \neq 0\), then \(d \neq 0\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If \(d = 0\), then \(n = 0 \cdot k = 0\) by~\Cref{lem:0n}, contradicting
\(n \neq 0\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A nonzero multiple is at least the positive factor]
\label{lem:dvd-le}
If \(d \mid n\), \(d \neq 0\), and \(n \neq 0\), then \(d \le n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write \(n = d \cdot k\). Then \(k \neq 0\) lest \(n = 0\)
by~\eqref{eq:mul0}. Hence \(k = \Suc(k')\) for some \(k'\)
by~\Cref{lem:pred}, and
\[
  n = d \cdot \Suc(k') = (d \cdot k') + d = d + (d \cdot k')
\]
by~\eqref{eq:mulS} and~\Cref{lem:add-comm}. Thus \(d \le n\)
by~\Cref{def:order}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Strict inequality for proper factors]
\label{lem:proper-factor}
Suppose \(n = d \cdot k\) with \(d \neq 0\) and \(k \neq 0\). Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \(d < n\) if and only if \(k \neq 1\);
  \item \(k < n\) if and only if \(d \neq 1\), provided \(n \neq 0\).
\end{enumerate}
In particular, if \(n = d \cdot k\) with \(d > 1\) and \(k > 1\), then
\(1 < d < n\) and \(1 < k < n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i)~Since \(n = d \cdot k = d + d \cdot k'\) whenever
\(k = \Suc(k')\) (as in~\Cref{lem:dvd-le}), we have \(d = n\) if and
only if \(d \cdot k' = 0\). As \(d \neq 0\), the identity
\(d \cdot k' = 0\) forces \(k' = 0\) (prove by induction on \(k'\) that
\(d \neq 0\) and \(d \cdot k' = 0\) imply \(k' = 0\): trivial for
\(k' = 0\); if \(k' = \Suc(k'')\), then
\(d \cdot k' = (d \cdot k'') + d = 0\), impossible because
\(x + d = 0\) with \(d \neq 0\) contradicts~\Cref{ax:peano1} after
writing \(d = \Suc(d')\)). Thus \(d = n\) if and only if \(k' = 0\),
i.e., \(k = 1\). By trichotomy and~\Cref{lem:dvd-le},
\(d < n\) if and only if \(d \neq n\), i.e., \(k \neq 1\).

(ii)~Apply (i) with the roles of \(d\) and \(k\) reversed, using
\Cref{lem:mul-comm}.

For the last claim: \(d > 1\) and \(k > 1\) give \(d \neq 0\),
\(k \neq 0\), \(d \neq 1\), \(k \neq 1\). Then \(d < n\) and
\(k < n\) by (i)--(ii), while \(d > 1\) and \(k > 1\) are
hypotheses.
\end{proof}

\section{Primes, composites, and even numbers}
\label{sec:prime}

\begin{definition}[Prime number]
\label{def:prime}
A natural number \(p\) is \emph{prime} if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \(p > 1\), and
  \item for every \(d \in \N\), if \(d \mid p\), then \(d = 1\) or
        \(d = p\).
\end{enumerate}
We write \(\IsPrime(p)\) to assert that \(p\) is prime.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Composite number]
\label{def:composite}
A natural number \(n\) is \emph{composite} if \(n > 1\) and \(n\) is not
prime. Equivalently (by~\Cref{def:prime}), \(n\) is composite if and only
if \(n > 1\) and there exists \(d \in \N\) such that \(d \mid n\),
\(d \neq 1\), and \(d \neq n\).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:composite-factorization}
By~\Cref{lem:dvd-pos,lem:proper-factor}, a number \(n > 1\) is composite
if and only if there exist \(d,k \in \N\) with \(n = d \cdot k\),
\(1 < d < n\), and \(1 < k < n\).
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Even number]
\label{def:even}
A natural number \(n\) is \emph{even} if \(2 \mid n\). We write
\(\IsEven(n)\) for this assertion. Equivalently, \(n\) is even if and
only if there exists \(k \in \N\) such that \(n = 2 \cdot k\).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Zero and one are not prime]
\label{lem:0-1-not-prime}
Neither \(0\) nor \(1\) is prime.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(v) and~\Cref{lem:0neq1}, one has \(0 < 1\), so
it is false that \(0 > 1\). Likewise it is false that \(1 > 1\). In both
cases clause~(i) of~\Cref{def:prime} fails.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Characterization of primes]
\label{lem:prime-char}
Let \(n \in \N\). Then \(n\) is prime if and only if \(n > 1\) and there
is no \(d \in \N\) with \(1 < d < n\) and \(d \mid n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose \(n\) is prime and \(1 < d < n\). If \(d \mid n\), then
\(d = 1\) or \(d = n\) by~\Cref{def:prime}, contradicting
\(1 < d < n\). Conversely, suppose \(n > 1\) and no divisor of \(n\)
lies strictly between \(1\) and \(n\). Let \(d \mid n\). If \(d = 0\),
then \(n = 0\) by~\Cref{lem:0n}, contradicting \(n > 1\). So \(d \neq 0\),
hence \(d \ge 1\). By~\Cref{lem:dvd-le} (applicable since \(n \neq 0\)),
we have \(d \le n\). By trichotomy, \(d = 1\), or \(1 < d < n\), or
\(d = n\). The middle alternative is forbidden by hypothesis. Thus
\(d = 1\) or \(d = n\), so \(n\) is prime.
\end{proof}

% ============================== 7 ==============================
\section{Preparatory lemmas}
\label{sec:prep}

\begin{lemma}[Divisors of two]
\label{lem:divisors-of-two}
If \(d \mid 2\), then \(d = 1\) or \(d = 2\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since \(2 \neq 0\), \Cref{lem:dvd-pos} yields \(d \neq 0\), and
\Cref{lem:dvd-le} yields \(d \le 2\). By~\Cref{lem:lt-succ} and
trichotomy, \(d = 1\) or \(d = 2\) or \(d = 0\); the last is excluded.
(More explicitly: \(d \neq 0\), and if \(d < 2\) then \(d = 1\)
by~\Cref{lem:lt-succ}; if \(d \not< 2\) then \(d = 2\) because
\(d \le 2\).)
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Two is an even prime]
\label{prop:two}
The number \(2\) is prime and even.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Evenness: \(2 = 2 \cdot 1\) by~\Cref{lem:n1}, so \(2 \mid 2\).
Primality: \(2 > 1\) because \(1 < 2\) by~\Cref{lem:order-basic}(iv). If
\(d \mid 2\), then \(d = 1\) or \(d = 2\)
by~\Cref{lem:divisors-of-two}. Hence \(\IsPrime(2)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Prime divides prime]
\label{lem:prime-dvd-prime}
If \(p\) and \(q\) are prime and \(p \mid q\), then \(p = q\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By~\Cref{def:prime} applied to \(q\), we have \(p = 1\) or \(p = q\).
But \(p > 1\) because \(p\) is prime, so \(p \neq 1\) by exclusivity of
trichotomy. Therefore \(p = q\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Halving an even number greater than two]
\label{lem:halve}
Suppose \(n > 2\) and \(n = 2 \cdot k\) for some \(k \in \N\). Then
\(1 < k < n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since \(n > 2 > 0\), we have \(n \neq 0\), so \(k \neq 0\)
by~\eqref{eq:mul0}. Moreover \(k \neq 1\): otherwise
\(n = 2 \cdot 1 = 2\) by~\Cref{lem:n1}, contradicting \(n > 2\). Thus
\(k > 1\) by~\Cref{lem:gt1-ge2} and trichotomy (since \(k \neq 0\) and
\(k \neq 1\)). Also \(2 > 1\). Apply~\Cref{lem:proper-factor} to obtain
\(k < n\), and we already have \(k > 1\).
\end{proof}

% ============================== 8 ==============================
\section{No even prime less than two}
\label{sec:below}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:below}
No natural number strictly less than \(2\) is prime. A fortiori, no even
natural number strictly less than \(2\) is prime.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(n < 2\). By~\Cref{lem:lt-succ}, \(n = 0\) or \(n = 1\). In either
case, \(n\) is not prime by~\Cref{lem:0-1-not-prime}. The second
assertion follows immediately: if \(n < 2\) and \(n\) were an even prime,
then \(n\) would be prime, which is impossible.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The hypothesis of evenness is idle in~\Cref{thm:below}. We retain it in
the ``a fortiori'' clause only to mirror the statement dual
to~\Cref{thm:above} below.
\end{remark}

\section{No even prime greater than two}
\label{sec:above}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:above}
If \(n > 2\) and \(n\) is even, then \(n\) is not prime. Equivalently:
every even natural number strictly greater than \(2\) is composite.
\end{theorem}

We record five proofs. Each uses only results established above.

\subsection{First proof: direct appeal to the definition}
\label{sec:proofA}

\begin{proof}[First proof of~\Cref{thm:above}]
It is equivalent (by taking the contrapositive in the prime variable,
and using~\Cref{prop:two} for the boundary case) to show: if \(p\) is
prime and even, then \(p = 2\). Let \(p\) be prime and even. Then
\(2 \mid p\) by~\Cref{def:even}. By~\Cref{def:prime}(ii), \(2 = 1\) or
\(2 = p\). The identity \(2 = 1\) is false by~\Cref{lem:0neq1}.
Therefore \(p = 2\). Consequently, if \(n > 2\) is even, then \(n\)
cannot be prime.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Second proof: contrapositive factorization}
\label{sec:proofB}

\begin{proof}[Second proof of~\Cref{thm:above}]
Let \(n > 2\) be even. By~\Cref{def:even}, there exists \(k \in \N\)
with \(n = 2 \cdot k\). By~\Cref{lem:halve}, \(1 < k < n\). In
particular \(k \neq 1\) and \(k \neq n\), while \(k \mid n\) because
\(n = k \cdot 2\) by~\Cref{lem:mul-comm}. Also \(n > 1\) because
\(n > 2 > 1\). Thus \(n\) fails~\Cref{def:prime}(ii), so \(n\) is not
prime. Since \(n > 1\), \(n\) is composite
by~\Cref{def:composite}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Third proof: contradiction}
\label{sec:proofC}

\begin{proof}[Third proof of~\Cref{thm:above}]
Suppose toward a contradiction that \(n > 2\) is both even and prime.
Then \(2 \mid n\). By~\Cref{def:prime}(ii), \(2 = 1\) or \(2 = n\). The
first alternative contradicts~\Cref{lem:0neq1}. The second contradicts
\(n > 2\) by exclusivity of trichotomy. This exhausts the
alternatives.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Fourth proof: via divisibility of primes}
\label{sec:proofD}

\begin{proof}[Fourth proof of~\Cref{thm:above}]
Let \(n > 2\) be even, and suppose for contradiction that \(n\) is
prime. Then \(2 \mid n\). Since \(2\) is prime by~\Cref{prop:two},
\Cref{lem:prime-dvd-prime} yields \(2 = n\), contradicting \(n > 2\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Fifth proof: least counterexample}
\label{sec:proofE}

\begin{proof}[Fifth proof of~\Cref{thm:above}]
Suppose there exists an even prime greater than \(2\). Let \(P(n)\) be
the property ``\(n\) is even, \(n > 2\), and \(n\) is prime.'' By
hypothesis \(P\) holds for some natural number, so~\Cref{cor:least-witness}
furnishes a least \(m\) such that \(P(m)\) holds. In particular \(m\)
is even and \(m > 2\). By the factorization argument of the second
proof (which uses only~\Cref{lem:halve} and the definitions, and makes
no minimality demand), \(m\) is not prime. This contradicts \(P(m)\).
Therefore no even prime greater than \(2\) exists.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
As the fifth proof itself notes, minimality is not essential to the
arithmetic step: every even integer greater than \(2\) is composite by
the second proof. The least-counterexample organization is included as
an independent classical pattern, resting on~\Cref{thm:wellorder}.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:above}
If \(p\) is prime and even, then \(p = 2\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By~\Cref{def:prime}, \(p > 1\), so \(p \ge 2\)
by~\Cref{lem:gt1-ge2}. If \(p > 2\), then~\Cref{thm:above} asserts that
\(p\) is not prime, a contradiction. By~\Cref{lem:trichotomy},
\(p = 2\).
\end{proof}

\section{The uniqueness theorem}
\label{sec:grand}

\begin{definition}[Expansive even-prime picture]
\label{def:expansive}
The \emph{expansive even-prime picture} is the conjunction of the
following four assertions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item there is no even prime \(n < 2\);
  \item there is no even prime \(n > 2\);
  \item \(2\) is prime;
  \item \(2\) is even.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of the even prime]
\label{thm:grand}
The following statements are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item The expansive even-prime picture holds.
  \item For every \(p \in \N\), if \(p\) is prime and even, then
        \(p = 2\).
  \item The number \(2\) is prime and even, and it is the only even
        prime in \(\N\).
  \item There does not exist \(p \in \N\) such that \(p\) is prime,
        \(p\) is even, and \(p \neq 2\).
\end{enumerate}
In particular, each of~(i)--(iv) is true.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We first show that~(i) holds, then establish the equivalences.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Verification of~(i).}
Clause~(a) is~\Cref{thm:below}. Clause~(b) is~\Cref{thm:above}.
Clauses~(c) and~(d) are~\Cref{prop:two}.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(i)\,\(\Rightarrow\)\,(ii).}
Assume~(i), and let \(p\) be an even prime. By~\Cref{lem:trichotomy},
exactly one of \(p < 2\), \(p = 2\), or \(p > 2\) holds. The case
\(p < 2\) is forbidden by~(a). The case \(p > 2\) is forbidden by~(b).
Therefore \(p = 2\).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(ii)\,\(\Rightarrow\)\,(i).}
Clauses~(c) and~(d) are~\Cref{prop:two}. Clause~(a)
is~\Cref{thm:below}, which did not use~(ii). For~(b): if \(n > 2\) were
an even prime, then~(ii) would force \(n = 2\), contradicting
\(n > 2\).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(ii)\,\(\Rightarrow\)\,(iii).}
Immediate from~\Cref{prop:two} together with~(ii).

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(iii)\,\(\Rightarrow\)\,(iv).}
If some even prime \(p\) satisfied \(p \neq 2\), uniqueness in~(iii)
would fail.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{(iv)\,\(\Rightarrow\)\,(ii).}
Let \(p\) be an even prime. If \(p \neq 2\), we contradict~(iv).
Hence \(p = 2\).

\medskip
\noindent Since~(i) holds and~(i) through~(iv) are equivalent, all four
statements are true.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:unique}
The unique even prime natural number is \(2\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from~\Cref{thm:grand}(iii).
\end{proof}
